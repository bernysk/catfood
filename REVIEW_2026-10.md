## TL;DR

1. **The pipeline reproduces exactly.** Rebuilding gives a byte-identical dataset. The audit shows only the known Royal Canin false positive, and every parsed number appears literally in the source text. Extraction is solid.
2. **There is one real scoring bug.** All 9 **Ontario (CZ)** products are complementary, but they score as "not stated" (1 pt instead of 0). The cause is that `curated.json` contains the Slovak wordings ("Doplnkové krmivo pre…") but not the Czech ones ("Doplňkové krmivo pro…"). The CZ rows became canonical later. `build_dataset.py` even prints this warning on every run.
   *Effect:* the Ontario mean drops from 68.8 to **60.0**, and 2 products fall from B to C. The overall distribution becomes 33 A / **53 B / 60 C** / 4 D / 1 E.
3. **Several numbers in the document are stale and contradict `dataset.json`.** Details are in §1.3.
4. **The biggest methodology issues:**
   - Grades are fragile under EU label tolerances. Under legal tolerances, only 73 % of grades survive in the median product.
   - A tiny-dose prebiotic flag is the single most influential criterion.
   - Taurine is scored from the *added* dose, against a threshold below the FEDIAF minimum for neutered cats.
   - Nutrients are compared per kg of dry matter instead of per 1000 kcal.
   - Limitation 9.1 misdescribes EU labels: they are declared values with tolerances, not min/max guarantees.
5. **High protein is not shown to damage healthy cat kidneys.** That includes neutered females, which are, if anything, at *lower* CKD risk than neutered males. The kidney-relevant nutrient is **phosphorus**, especially soluble inorganic phosphate. For a neutered indoor adult, ~**110 g protein / 1000 kcal or more** is what lean-mass data support. Healthy seniors should **not** be protein-restricted. Restriction belongs to diagnosed CKD (IRIS 2+) or proteinuria, under a vet.

## 2. Methodology review

Ranked by how much each issue changes the conclusions.

### 2.1 Grades are within label noise

Under EU Reg. 767/2009 Annex IV (as amended by 2017/2279), declared analytical constituents are **values with tolerances**, not min/max guarantees. That min/max model is the US "guaranteed analysis". Limitation 9.1 in the document should be corrected.

For feed for non-food-producing animals, with declared content under 16 %, the tolerances are:

- protein: **±2 units**
- fat: −2 / +4 units
- fibre: ±1.75 units
- ash: −2 / +1 units

A pouch declaring 8 % protein at 80 % moisture can legally contain 6–10 %, which is **30–50 % DM**. That spans all three protein bands of the framework.

Monte Carlo, 2 000 draws per product, perturbing the five label values (moisture ±1):

| Scenario | Median share of draws keeping the grade | Products < 80 % stable | < 60 % stable |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| "typical" noise (protein/fat ±0.5, ash ±0.3, fibre ±0.2) | 94 % | 45 / 148 | 9 |
| EU legal tolerance | **73 %** | 93 / 148 | **55** |

The least stable products are mostly Brit Premium and Brit Care (14 each), Sheba (6), Calibra and CarniLove (5 each). These sit right on a protein or NFE band edge.

**Recommendation:** publish a stability figure next to each grade (e.g. "B (68 % stable)"). Alternatively, show score ranges instead of single letters. Avoid ranking products whose intervals overlap.

### 2.2 Prebiotic flag is the most influential criterion, not the best-evidenced

Leave-one-criterion-out analysis:

| Criterion dropped | Spearman ρ with published ranking | Grades that change |
|---|---:|---:|
| prebiotic | **0.864** | **71 / 151** |
| completeness | 0.921 | 51 |
| clean label | 0.947 | 43 |
| protein | 0.960 | 34 |
| carbs (NFE) | 0.939 | 32 |
| taurine | 0.960 | 26 |
| named meat | 0.899 | 14 |

The document itself (§3.1–3.3, §7.5) argues that 0.1–0.4 % inulin is "real but modest". Studies used 6 % psyllium or 11–15 % total fibre. Yet the mere presence of the word *inulín/čakanka* is worth as much as the entire protein criterion, and it moves more grades than anything else.

There is also an internal inconsistency. Psyllium is described as *low-fermentable* (§3.2) but is scored in the "fermentable" class.

**Options:**
- Down-weight the prebiotic criterion (e.g. 0–1).
- Score it on declared dose.
- Move it to a filterable attribute, like texture.

### 2.3 Taurine: added dose ≠ content, and the threshold is below the FEDIAF minimum for neutered cats

- The `additives_per_kg_raw` field declares **supplemented** taurine (nutritional additive 3a370). Muscle meat, heart and fish supply additional taurine that the label doesn't show. A low declared figure means "low supplement", not "deficient". The Stuzzy 100 mg/kg finding is still worth a note, but not a deficiency claim.
- FEDIAF 2025 Table III-4a, minimum taurine for **canned** food:

  | Cat | mg/kg DM |
  |---|---:|
  | adult at 100 kcal/kg^0.67 (active) | 2 000 |
  | growth | 2 500 |
  | **adult at 75 kcal/kg^0.67 (neutered/indoor)** | **2 700** |

  The framework gives full points at ≥ 2 500 mg/kg DM. **15 products fall in the 2 500–2 699 band.** They get 2/2 even though they sit under the neutered-adult starting point.
- On an energy basis (FEDIAF Table III-4b, 670 mg/1000 kcal for neutered adults), 48 of 92 complete foods with declared taurine are below that figure from the *added* taurine alone. Most likely they are adequate via natural taurine. FEDIAF calls its taurine values "starting points", validated by blood levels.

### 2.4 Use energy basis (per 1000 kcal), not only dry matter

FEDIAF's DM tables assume 4 000 kcal/kg DM. Wet pouches vary from ~3 300 to 5 500 kcal/kg DM, mostly because fat varies. Per-DM comparisons therefore reward low-fat foods.

What the cat actually eats is calories, so **g per 1000 kcal ME** is the comparable unit. `reanalysis.py` computes it for every row:

- declared ME where available (58 rows)
- otherwise the modified Atwater estimate, 3.5 / 8.5 / 3.5 kcal/g, which runs ~8 % below declared values

Results across DM-reliable rows:

| Measure | Median | Range |
|---|---:|---:|
| protein, g/1000 kcal | 113 | 60–159 |
| protein, % of energy | 41 % | 21–59 % |
| fat, % of energy | 47 % | 21–67 % |
| carbohydrate (NFE), % of energy | 12 % | 2–30 % |

For reference, cats self-select ~52 % protein / 36 % fat / 12 % carbohydrate by energy (Hewson-Hughes 2011). The market here is **fat-heavy and protein-moderate**, not carbohydrate-heavy. That argues for giving the NFE criterion less weight than "protein per kcal".

### 2.5 Smaller points

- **Complementary foods should not get a grade on the same scale.** They aren't meant to be balanced, so a "C" for a treat and a "C" for a complete food mean different things. Exclude them from the A–E ranking and brand means, and list them separately.
- **"Not stated" = 1 pt for 67 products.** That is 44 % of the dataset sitting on a neutral score whose real value is binary. Again, it is better to report it as unknown than to score it.
- **Grain penalty.** There is no evidence that rice or wheat in small amounts harms cats. Sugar (usually caramel colour in gravy) is a better-justified flag. Splitting "clean label" into the two flags would be more honest.
- **Renormalising over missing criteria** compares products scored out of 10 points with others scored out of 14. Showing *n criteria scored* per product would make that visible.
- **Phosphorus is collected but not used** (43 products declare it). It is the kidney-relevant nutrient, so consider adding it, per 1000 kcal, as an informational column (§4).

## 3. Research: is high protein bad for cat kidneys?

### 3.1 Healthy cats: no evidence of harm

- **Finco et al. 1998 (AJVR 59:575)** used a 2×2 protein × calorie design in cats with surgically reduced renal mass. After 12 months, **only high calorie intake** worsened renal lesions. High protein did not increase lesions, proteinuria or GFR decline.
- This resolved the confound in **Adams et al. 1993/1994**, where the high-protein group also ate more calories.
- **Polzin et al. 2000 (JFMS)** reviewed the studies above: excessive protein does not appear to cause renal failure in healthy cats.
- No controlled trial shows that a high-protein diet *causes* CKD in healthy cats. The one long-term high-protein study found was industry-run, with 20 cats over 2 years (Virbac). It found no decline.

### 3.2 The nutrient that does affect kidneys: phosphorus, especially soluble inorganic phosphate

- **Alexander et al. 2019 (Br J Nutr 121:249).** Healthy adult cats were fed diets enriched with sodium dihydrogen phosphate.
  - At ~3.6 g inorganic P/1000 kcal, GFR fell within 4 weeks and the study was halted.
  - At ~1.5 g/1000 kcal, **36 %** of cats developed altered renal echogenicity over 29 weeks.
- **Coltherd et al. 2021 (Br J Nutr 126:1626).** 1 g P/1000 kcal from sodium tripolyphosphate, with total P up to 5 g/1000 kcal, caused no adverse kidney or bone effects over 30 weeks.
- **FEDIAF 2025, footnote f:** "≥ 1.5 g/1000 kcal of highly bioavailable inorganic P can affect indicators of kidney function in cats". About 1 g/1000 kcal has been fed without detectable harm, including a 5-year study in aged cats (Reynolds et al. 2024, JVIM). FEDIAF still sets **no** formal maximum for cats.
- **AAFCO expert panel (2025):** suggests 5 g total P/1000 kcal as an interim maximum.
- **Summers et al. 2020 (JVIM):** analysed P was often *higher* than declared minimums. Many foods had high P and low Ca.

**In your data**, the 43 products that declare P range from **0.9 to 3.1 g/1000 kcal** with Ca:P of 1.0–1.6. That is well under the 5 g interim maximum, with no inverted ratios. Only one product names an inorganic phosphate (Royal Canin Digestive Care US: tripolyphosphate). However, EU labels may group phosphates under "minerály", so absence from the label proves nothing.

### 3.3 Cats with CKD: restriction helps, but mostly through phosphorus

- **Ross et al. 2006 (JAVMA 229:949).** Double-masked RCT in 45 cats with IRIS stage 2–3 CKD. A renal diet (restricted P and protein, modified Na and lipids) gave **0 % uraemic crises vs 26 %** on maintenance food, and fewer renal deaths. The diets differed in several nutrients at once, so the effect of protein cannot be isolated.
- **Schauf et al. 2021 (JVIM 35:2797, Mars-funded).** 19 cats with IRIS 1–2 CKD.
  - A *strongly* restricted diet (59 g protein, 0.84 g P/1000 kcal) caused **hypercalcaemia**.
  - A *moderate* diet (76–98 g protein, 1.4–1.6 g P/1000 kcal) kept renal markers stable and improved Ca–P balance.
  - Conclusion: early CKD may benefit from **moderate**, not severe, restriction.
- **IRIS (2023):** renal diet from stage 2. Serum phosphate targets are 0.9–1.45 mmol/L at stage 2, 0.9–1.6 at stage 3 and 0.9–1.9 at stage 4. Protein reduction is recommended for **proteinuric** cats at any stage.
- **Counterpoint:** weight and lean-mass loss predict shorter survival in CKD cats. Over-restricting protein can harm, so restriction should be vet-managed, not a DIY choice.

### 3.4 Neutered female cats specifically

- **Kidney risk.** The largest risk-factor study (Greene et al. 2014, JAVMA, 1 230 cats) found **neutered males** at higher risk than spayed females. A 201-cat cohort found no sex effect. Neither supports elevated CKD risk in spayed females. The consistent risk factors are:
  - age
  - thin body condition
  - dental disease
  - prior cystitis
  - dehydration or anaesthesia
- **The real issue after spaying is energy, not kidneys.** Neutered cats eat more and need less energy:
  - FEDIAF uses 75 kcal/kg^0.67 for neutered/indoor cats, with a range of 52–75, versus 100 for active cats.
  - Optimal body condition for neutered inactive cats is **BCS 4/9**, not 5/9 (Bjornvad 2011).
  - For example, a 4 kg spayed indoor female needs only ~190 kcal/day.
- **Why that *raises* the protein target.** A cat eating fewer calories gets less protein from the same food. FEDIAF's neutered-adult column is therefore higher: **83.3 g protein/1000 kcal** (33.3 % DM) vs 62.5 for active cats.
- **Lean mass.** Laflamme & Hannah 2013 (JFMS) found cats needed **≥ 5.2 g protein/kg body weight/day** to *maintain lean body mass*, versus 1.5 g/kg for nitrogen balance. The study used neutered males, and the authors flag sex as untested.
  - For a 4 kg cat at 190 kcal, 5.2 g/kg needs ≈ **110 g protein/1000 kcal**.
  - At the FEDIAF minimum (83.3) she would get only ~3.9 g/kg.
- **Weight loss.** For overweight spayed cats, higher protein (~45 % vs 35 % of energy) increased fat loss and preserved lean mass (Laflamme & Hannah 2005, Purina-funded).

## 4. What protein level is best, by life stage

These are evidence-informed ranges, not an official standard. Wet-food DM figures assume ~4 000–4 500 kcal/kg DM.

| Life stage | FEDIAF 2025 minimum | Evidence-informed target | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kitten** (growth, to ~12 months) | 70–75 g/1000 kcal (28–30 % DM) | **100–140 g/1000 kcal** (~40–55 % DM); food must be *complete* and labelled for growth | Energy needs are 1.5–2.5× adult MER. Ca:P around 1–1.5. Growth foods need more Ca and P, so no phosphorus restriction. Complementary "kitten" pouches (e.g. Ontario Kitten) are not a diet. |
| **Adult, neutered/indoor** (esp. spayed females) | **83.3 g/1000 kcal** (33.3 % DM) | **≥ 110 g/1000 kcal**, comfortably 110–150 (~45–60 % DM) | Lean-mass data (5.2 g/kg/day) plus low calorie intake. Control *calories* (BCS 4/9), not protein. P at the lower end of the market range (≤ ~2–2.5 g/1000 kcal) with Ca:P ≥ 1 is a reasonable precaution, not a proven need. |
| **Adult, active/intact** | 62.5 g/1000 kcal (25 % DM) | ≥ 90–100 g/1000 kcal | More calories eaten, so more protein delivered at the same density. |
| **Healthy senior** (≥ ~11 y) | same as adult | **Do not restrict: ≥ 110–130 g/1000 kcal**, highly digestible, more energy-dense if losing weight | Protein and fat digestibility decline in a subset of cats over ~12 years. Sarcopenia is common. Prophylactic protein restriction is not supported. Screen annually or twice a year: SDMA + creatinine, UPC, blood pressure, BCS/muscle score. |
| **CKD, IRIS 1–2, non-proteinuric** | — | vet-guided **moderate** restriction (Schauf: ~76–98 g protein, 1.4–1.6 g P/1000 kcal) | Avoid severe P restriction (hypercalcaemia risk). Track weight and muscle. |
| **CKD, IRIS 2–4, or proteinuric** | — | **veterinary renal diet** (e.g. Purina NF in your data: 60 g protein, 0.9 g P/1000 kcal) | Strongest evidence is for the complete renal diet (Ross 2006). Add phosphate binders if serum P is above the IRIS target. |

**How the dataset compares.** Of 121 complete or "not stated" foods with reliable DM figures:

| Protein threshold | Foods meeting it |
|---|---:|
| ≥ 83.3 g/1000 kcal (neutered minimum) | 119 |
| ≥ 110 g/1000 kcal | 66 |
| ≥ 130 g/1000 kcal | 20 |

The only food below the neutered minimum is the renal vet diet (the low-protein Almo Nature product is dry kibble). Typical SK/CZ premium pouches therefore already sit in the target band for neutered adults and seniors. The usual risk is **too many calories** (spayed females) or **too little intake** (seniors), not too much protein.

**A note on blood tests.** High-protein meals raise urea and, slightly, creatinine. Creatinine also *falls* as muscle is lost, which flatters a sarcopenic senior. **SDMA** is less affected by muscle mass, so ask for it alongside creatinine.

## 5. Suggested additions to the study

1. **Fix the Ontario bug.** Make unknown completeness statements a hard error, and regenerate the doc's tables from `dataset.json`.
2. **Add an energy-basis layer** (`reanalysis.py` already computes it). Add "protein g/1000 kcal", "P g/1000 kcal", "Ca:P" and "% energy P/F/C" columns and filters to `index.html`.
3. **Add a life-stage lens** instead of one universal grade:
   - **kitten:** complete + growth-labelled, protein ≥ 100 g/1000 kcal
   - **neutered adult:** protein ≥ 110 g/1000 kcal, moderate energy density
   - **senior:** protein ≥ 110 g/1000 kcal, P ≤ ~2 g/1000 kcal where declared
4. **Publish uncertainty.** Show grade stability under EU tolerances (Monte Carlo in `reanalysis.py`).
5. **Re-weight or re-scope** the prebiotic and grain criteria (§2.2, §2.5). Exclude complementary foods from grading.
6. **Correct limitation 9.1** (EU tolerances, not min/max). Add "declared taurine = supplemented taurine".
7. **Add a short kidney/phosphorus section** to §3, based on §3 of this review. It is the most-asked owner question, and the current framework silently implies "more protein is always better" without saying why that is safe.
8. **Optional:** ask manufacturers for P and Ca on the ~100 products that don't declare them. Brit and CarniLove already publish them, so it's a reasonable request.
